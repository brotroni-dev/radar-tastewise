Radar

Agents that notice what matters, so Maya doesn’t have to know what to ask.

The prototype focuses on one moment: an update reaching Maya, and what she does next.

Roni Brot-Kolinkó
01 · The problem

The platform has the answers. Maya doesn’t have time to ask.

The platform can answer almost any question, but Maya has to start the conversation. She’s a brand manager, not an analyst, and she rarely opens the product. The challenge is to make the product useful before she thinks to open it.

Push should mean more than sending Maya notifications. It should mean the product keeping watch for her, noticing what matters and bringing it to her while there is still time to act.

Meet Maya

Brand Manager at a mid-sized CPG company, responsible for five natural supplement SKUs.

Measured on

Sales across her existing range, and whether new products succeed.

Her day

Meetings, email, spreadsheets and decks. She isn’t an analyst and doesn’t spend her day in the product.

How she uses it today

About twice a month, usually for a quarterly review, retailer meeting or launch brief.

How her work runs

Daily: meetings, approvals and questions from the team.
Weekly: performance reviews, status updates and management reporting.
Periodic: retailer reviews, quarterly planning and launch briefs.

What she needs

Walk into retailer meetings with data she can defend. Choose the next launch and explain why. Spot important changes before they become surprises.

What’s missing

She has to know what to ask before the product can help. The most valuable signals may be the ones she hasn’t noticed yet, while her team is waiting for answers.

The bet

Radar

Radar gives Maya a set of agents that keep watch for her. She describes what matters in her own words, and they bring her updates in Slack or email, timed to her daily work, weekly reporting and periodic planning. Each update arrives with a next step already drafted. Only a time-sensitive closing window interrupts that schedule, and it always comes with a deadline.

Cadence from the digestNoticing from alertsReadiness from the briefingTeam reach from the bot

Assumptions

  • The platform can build a brand profile from Maya's company and products.
  • Slack and email integrations exist.
  • Agents run on the platform's existing data and query capability.
  • Retailer-level share data is available for her SKUs.
  • Retailer calendars (resets, promo windows) are available, so a window can carry a date.
  • Her team can see what she shares without a license.
  • Nothing is sent to anyone without Maya.

Design principles

The decisions that shaped the prototype.

Start full, not empty

Five agents are on from day one, named the way Maya would say them. She switches off what she doesn’t need, or adds her own with a prompt.

Built around her work

Each agent follows one cadence: daily, weekly or periodic.

Only a closing window interrupts

It comes today, with a date. Nothing else breaks the rhythm.

Why it matters comes first

Every update opens with what it means for her brand, before any numbers.

Every number has a source

And a timeframe. She can put it in front of a buyer.

Ready, not raw

A slide, an email or a meeting, already drafted for her to review and send.

Control inside the message

Adjust and Not relevant are available inside the update, not buried in settings.

One card, every channel

The same update in Slack, in email and in the product.

It shows what it learned

Feedback shapes the next update, and Radar shows Maya what changed.

04 · Working with AI

What I asked for, where AI fell short, and what I decided.

I used ChatGPT and Gemini for background research, and Claude Code to plan and build. I made the product decisions and wrote most of the final copy. Claude handled the implementation, and I reviewed every round in the browser.

What I asked for

  • A plan and open questions before any code.
  • A full copy draft to review before the build.
  • Short rounds: build, review in the browser, refine.

Where AI fell short

  • Its first concept, a pre-meeting briefing, was too narrow.
  • It proposed a metric the product couldn’t measure.
  • Its copy read as generated. I rewrote most of it.
  • It treated cadence as a setting and missed urgency entirely.

What I decided

  • Reframed the concept twice, away from the meeting.
  • Added Maya’s three rhythms and the closing window.
  • Put feedback inside the update, not in settings.
  • Kept the scope to one piece: the update and what she does next.
05 · What I'd measure

Three numbers that would tell me it worked.

Habit
Weekly active use, up from roughly two sessions a month, plus update open rate by channel.
Insight to action
Share of updates that lead to a slide, email or meeting.
Signal quality
Percentage of agents still active after 30 days, with the Not relevant rate decreasing over time.

What I’d test next

  • Whether Maya trusts updates she didn’t ask for.
  • How many updates a week feel useful before they feel like noise.
  • Whether closing-window dates are reliable enough to interrupt her.
